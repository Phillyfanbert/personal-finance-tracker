Help

A short guided tour runs automatically the first time you open each page, pointing at each part in turn. It only runs once per page per device, and finishing or skipping it counts as done - use the button below to see it again.

This page has three tabs, and the three totals at the top stay on screen whichever one you are looking at. Spending is where you write things down, Bills is anything that charges you on a schedule, and Money is what you have and what you owe. A budget-warning banner can appear above the tabs when a category is close to or over its limit. Tap any row above to jump to what it does.

Type a whole expense as free text, e.g. "$14 lunch chipotle debit" - amount, merchant, and a matching account (if you name a payment type like "debit" or a bank name) are detected automatically as you type. This always works with no setup (a built-in keyword parser). If this household's optional Gemma server is configured (GEMMA_ENDPOINT), a second pass runs a moment later and can fill in fields the keyword parser missed or got less precisely - you'll see "Asking Gemma..." then "Parsed by Gemma" in small text under the field. Nothing is saved until you tap Save.

Four fields are required before Save will go through: Amount, Account, Category, Date. Any of the four that's still empty gets a live red border the whole time, not just after a rejected Save - it clears the instant you fill it in. Description/merchant is the only optional field.

If the account it guessed is wrong (or it couldn't guess), just pick the right one from the Account dropdown yourself - it never blocks you, it just doesn't guess when it isn't confident (for example, two Chase accounts and the text only says "chase").

Import CSV (the small button under the box) brings in a file from your bank or another app, in four steps: pick the file, check the columns, look over what it found, then confirm. The file never leaves your device.

It reads the column names and fills the settings in for you, so usually there is nothing to change. It handles both shapes of bank file: one where a single amount column goes negative for money spent, and one with separate money-out and money-in columns. When it spots the second kind it uses those two columns and stops asking about signs, because the column already says which direction the money went.

One file can bring in both what you spent and what you were paid. "What is in this file?" is set to read both by default, which is right for a normal bank statement. Change it to one or the other if your file only holds one kind, and anything pointing the other way is skipped instead of being brought in backwards. Money you received is shown in green with a plus sign so you can tell the two apart before confirming.

Everything imported is treated as history that has already happened, so it never changes an account balance - your balance in real life already includes it, and counting it again would double it. If you picked the wrong file or the wrong columns, "Undo this import" takes the whole lot back out, both the spending and the income.

A combined feed of every expense and every non-expense money movement (a manual balance correction, a liability payment, a transfer between your own accounts, income received, an investment contribution, a logged interest charge, even an account being opened) - not just expenses. Search by merchant/description text, or narrow with the Type/Account/Category/Kind dropdowns and a min/max dollar range - all filters combine (AND, not OR). "Kind" is the one that separates plain expenses from everything else (account activity has no category or payment type of its own, so it drops out of those two filters automatically unless you pick "Activity only").

Tap an expense row to open it for editing or recategorizing. Account-activity rows (anything that isn't a plain expense) can't be edited that way since most of them have no separate edit form - instead every row, expense or activity, has a small undo icon that reverses exactly what it did (restores the old balance, removes the row) and can't be used twice.

Check the box on several expense rows at once to recategorize all of them together in one Apply - your checked selection survives changing the filters above it, so you can search, check a few, search again, and check more before applying.

Assets total minus liabilities total (tracked debts only) - a single number, read-only here. It deliberately does not subtract subscriptions or general spending: a subscription is a recurring future cost, not a debt you owe today, and ordinary spending already reduced an asset's balance directly the moment you logged it. See the Reports page for how this number trends over time.

Payment methods only - which card, bank, or wallet you paid with. Accounts are not counted in net worth by themselves; every account automatically gets its own linked Asset (a debit/deposit-style account) or Liability (a credit-style account) behind the scenes, and that linked balance is what actually counts. "+ Add" walks you through picking a type (this decides the account's name, like Checking or Credit Card) and typing the bank or institution name - for a real bank/credit union this is checked against a large built-in list as you type, and if it's genuinely not on the list you're offered an "add it anyway" option rather than being blocked outright.

Tap any account circle to adjust its balance directly: type the new total, or use +/- if you only know the change. A credit-type account instead shows Pay/adjust-owed controls from the Liabilities card below, plus a credit-utilization line and (once you've filled in its statement-cycle details there) a paid-in-full / carrying-a-balance / missed-minimum status. Archiving an account (the small "Archive" link under a circle) removes it from every list and total while keeping its full history intact and fully reversible from "View archived" - unlike the ✕ delete, which is permanent and unassigns its past expenses.

Each account type shows its real-world opening requirement under the type picker - most need you to be 18, but plenty don't, and a few work differently than people expect. An IRA has no minimum age at all (it needs earned income instead, so a teenager with a job can hold one); a custodial UTMA/UGMA works the other way around, where the beneficiary has to be a minor; and 401(k)-style minimums are set by the employer's plan rather than by law. If your profile's birth year suggests you'd be under the usual age, the app asks you to confirm rather than refusing - it records accounts you already have, so a custodial account, a joint account, or one opened years ago all need to go in. With no birth year saved, it never asks at all.

Transfer (button next to "+ Add") moves money between two of your own accounts - Checking to Savings, for example. Only accounts with a real balance behind them can be picked (not a credit card or loan - moving money onto a card is paying it down, which stays on the Liabilities card's own Pay flow below). Shows up in Recent History as a single undoable entry, and reduces the "from" account's balance the moment it's confirmed the same way any other spending does.

Everything you own that counts toward net worth - not just accounts. "+ Add asset" covers standalone things with no linked account: an Investment, Property, Vehicle, a retirement account you're tracking on its own, or any of roughly fifty specific types (brokerage, 401(k)/Roth/Traditional variants, HSA, crypto, and more). A Vehicle gets an optional depreciation estimate (purchase price, date, and a yearly rate) instead of you updating its value by hand. An investment-flavored asset gets an optional cost basis and allocation bucket, both used on the Investments page; adding a ticker/symbol and quantity here also lets the app price it automatically from the live market feed, so you never update that value by hand.

Investments appear here as a single "Investments" line, not one row per account, and its figure is the same total the Investments page shows at the top - the two are calculated the same way and cannot drift apart. Tap the line to go straight to that page, where the per-account and per-ticker detail lives. Keeping it to one line here is deliberate: investments have a whole page of their own, and listing every brokerage and retirement account again on this page split the same information across two places.

A CD's maturity date shows an upcoming-renewal reminder here once it's within 30 days. Any manually-priced asset (no live feed) that hasn't been touched in 6+ months gets a gentle "this might be stale" notice, just as a reminder to check it's still accurate - nothing changes automatically.

Tracked debt only, split into two groups: Credit liabilities are the automatic ones tied to a credit-type Account above (their balance moves only through real charges/payments, or a manual correction - see below); Other liabilities are standalone ones you add directly here (Loan, Mortgage, or a specific type like a HELOC or student loan) with no linked account.

"Pay" on any liability moves money from a linked asset/account you pick straight to paying it down. The balance can also be corrected directly - set it to an exact figure, or add a specific charge - which is useful when the real statement doesn't quite match what got logged (a fee, a late-arriving charge, a refund); every one of these corrections is written to Recent History as an undoable entry, so nothing changes the amount owed silently. A payment larger than the balance is refused rather than accepted and floored at zero, the same way a real card rejects an overpayment - the app has no concept of a credit balance to hold the difference in, so the extra would simply vanish from the funding account.

"Edit details" (small link under any liability) opens interest rate, minimum payment, and due date for any liability, plus type-specific fields: a credit limit for a card/HELOC/line of credit; statement-cycle fields (which day the statement closes, which day payment is due, the last statement balance and date) for a real revolving card, which power a paid-in-full / carrying-a-balance / missed-minimum indicator - paying the statement balance in full by the due date means no interest at all, carrying any of it accrues interest even if every payment was on time, and paying only the minimum avoids a late fee but not the interest. Nothing here is ever applied automatically; you confirm each interest charge yourself from the small "Log interest" link when it's ready. A HELOC additionally gets a draw-period-end date.

Setting a credit limit does two things. It adds a plain utilization line ("$X of $Y used") to the liability, deliberately with no "aim for under 30%" advice attached - just the math. And it becomes a real ceiling: once a limit is set, a new expense, subscription renewal or edit that would push the balance past it is refused with a message telling you how much room is left, the same way a real card declines. Pay the balance down and the room comes back.

Two deliberate exceptions. Correcting the amount owed by hand is never blocked, because a real balance genuinely can exceed its limit through an over-limit fee, interest, or the bank lowering your limit - if that happens you still need to be able to record the truth. And charge cards and buy-now-pay-later plans are never limited, because neither has a preset spending limit in real life. If no limit is set, nothing is enforced, since a blank field means "unknown", not "zero".

Source and Amount are required, and so are the two pay days if you pick semimonthly. Anything still needed is outlined in red while you fill the form in, and clears as soon as you type. Next expected and Deposited into are not required, but the app can only add money for you if it knows when and where - leave either blank and the source still counts toward your yearly income, it just never records a deposit. The form says so underneath those two fields rather than letting it fail quietly.

Recurring money coming in - a paycheck, and similar. Each source records its amount, cadence, which account it's deposited into, when the next one's expected, and an Active checkbox, then logs itself automatically once it comes due, the same way a bill charge does. Cadence covers weekly, biweekly, semimonthly (two fixed pay days a month, like the 1st and 15th - not just "every ~15 days," which would slowly drift off your real payday), monthly, annual, and one-time. A one-time source turns itself inactive right after it's logged, so it never logs twice.

Only accounts with a real balance behind them can be picked as the deposit account - not a credit card or loan, the same restriction Transfer uses above and for the same reason. Each deposit shows up in Recent History as "Income received," undoable like any other entry there.

The Income / yr tile at the top of this page annualizes every active recurring source. It reads $0 until you add one, with a line underneath saying whether that's because nothing is recorded yet or because your only source is one-time - a one-time deposit isn't a yearly rate, so it deliberately isn't counted toward the total. This figure is also what the app checks against the two account types where income is a real legal requirement (see Accounts above).

The sum of every active subscription/bill's amount, normalized to a month and a year regardless of each one's own billing cycle (a $120/year plan counts as $10/month here). This is a forecast based on what you've entered, not a count of real charges - a charge only becomes real, dated spending once you use Mark as paid (see below) or it shows up through Quick Add/CSV import like any other expense.

Only shown when your expense history has a pattern that looks like a bill (the same merchant, a similar amount, roughly the same interval). Tap one to start tracking it here as a real subscription/bill instead of just a repeating expense - nothing is created automatically without you tapping it.

Compares what you're paying against a small built-in catalog of plan types (student, military, family-plan, and similar eligible pricing) using your Profile's deal-eligibility fields (military/student/first-responder status, and so on) - fill those in on the Log page's Profile modal to get real matches here. The total next to the card title is what these checked plans would save you over a year.

Found online, not verified is a second part inside this same card, kept visually separate because it is a different level of trust. Everything above it comes from that checked list; everything in it was found automatically by a background search and nobody has confirmed it. It appears only when there is actually something to show, and its savings are deliberately left out of the total at the top of the card, which only ever counts plans the app can stand behind.

An optional feature: a background weekly search looks for live subscription pricing and writes candidate findings there, clearly labeled unverified with a link to check the source before you trust it - nothing is ever applied automatically.

You see at most one row per subscription: the cheapest thing found, and only when it genuinely costs less than what you already pay. The search often turns up the same plan several times from different pages, and it also finds prices that are the same as or higher than yours - none of that is a saving, so none of it is shown. Each row states the new monthly price against what you pay now and what that adds up to over a year.

If the source page advertises a free trial, that is called out on its own line ("3 months free first, then $6.99/mo"), because the headline price on those pages is what you pay after the trial ends, not now. Below it, "From the page" quotes the actual text the price was read out of, so every number here can be traced to something you can go and read yourself using the source link. A trial can only be shown when the page text was captured, which is not the case for every finding - so treat a row with no trial line as "not stated here" rather than "no trial exists", and check the source.

A small "Last updated" line shows how recently the search last ran. If that run only partly finished, a short grey note underneath says so, so a thin-looking list has an explanation rather than leaving you guessing. It is deliberately quiet: these background searches are best-effort and a partial run is ordinary, not a fault.

"+ Add" (top right, or at the bottom of the full list) opens a form: service name, a free-typed category (with suggestions from common ones, but you can type your own), amount, billing cycle (monthly/quarterly/semiannual/annual/other), next renewal date, which account it's paid from, an Active checkbox (inactive ones drop out of the Monthly/Yearly totals and upcoming renewals), an Essential checkbox (a bill you need, versus a discretionary subscription - recorded for your own reference), and notes.

Mark as paid (only shown once the subscription has an account linked and has been saved) logs today's date as a real, dated expense against that account - the exact same effect on your balances as typing it into Quick Add - and automatically rolls the next-renewal date forward by one billing cycle. Use it the day you're actually charged, not when you first set the subscription up.

Upcoming renewals lists anything due in the next 30 days, for a quick glance - it's read-only here, tap the full list below to actually edit one. All subscriptions/bills is the complete list, active and inactive both; tap any row to open it back in the edit form above.

Account security is at the top: your sign-in email (read-only) and a place to set or change your password - this is what actually keeps an installed home-screen icon signed in (a magic-link email always opens in regular Safari, which an installed icon can't reach). Below that, two optional groups: deal-eligibility details (military/student/first-responder status, and so on) used to match you against real subscription discounts on the Log page's Bills tab, and financial-context details (housing, employment, household size, goals) used only to give the Reports page's "Ask about your spending" feature something real to reason about - neither group is used anywhere else. "Download a copy of my data" saves a file holding everything in your account. It asks you to confirm first, because that file is readable by anyone who can open it - only do it on a device you trust.

Everything here works without a mouse. Tab moves between things, Enter or Space activates whatever is focused, and the thing you are on always has a visible outline so you can see where you are. That includes the small delete crosses, which used to be reachable only by tapping.

Any box that opens over the page - a confirmation, Profile, Import CSV, Help - can be closed three ways: its Close button, the Escape key, or tapping the dimmed area outside it. While one is open the page behind it is inactive, so you cannot lose your place in it, and closing it puts you back exactly where you were.

If a list fails to load, you get a red box saying so with a Try again button, rather than an empty space. That matters because an empty space looks the same as having no data, which would send you looking for the wrong problem. Anything the app tells you in the small message at the bottom of the screen is also read out by screen readers, and a rejected field is outlined in red with the reason written next to it.

Everything on this page is about what happens next, which is what separates it from Reports: a limit you are setting, an order you are weighing up, and a balance projected forward. Nothing here changes any of your balances on its own. Subscriptions and bills used to be this tab and now live under Log, with the rest of your spending.

A monthly spending limit per category. Unlike the charts on the Reports page, a budget always applies to the current real-world month - it doesn't follow any month picker. Once you're at 90% of a limit the bar turns amber and the figure beside it says "close to the limit"; over it, the bar turns red and it says "over". The words matter as much as the colour, so it still reads correctly if colours are hard to tell apart, and a "Budget warning" banner appears at the very top of this page (and a toast fires right at the moment a save actually crosses the line). Set a category's limit again to change it; tap the ✕ to remove it entirely.

If you owe money in more than one place, there are two common ways to decide where any spare money should go. One is to put it toward whichever debt charges the highest interest rate. The other is to put it toward whichever debt is smallest, so you clear one sooner. This card works out what each would actually cost you and shows both, side by side.

The box is for spare money you could put toward debt each month, on top of the smallest payment each one already requires. Leaving it at 0 is fine - the comparison still works, it just takes longer either way. For each option you get how many months until everything is paid off, and how much interest you would pay in total getting there.

It will never tell you which to choose. Both are reasonable, people pick differently for good reasons, and this app shows you the numbers rather than making that decision for you.

A debt is left out of the comparison if the app does not yet know its interest rate and smallest monthly payment, if it is already paid off, or if it is a home equity line you can still borrow more from (those work differently enough that including them would give a misleading answer). A short note under the numbers says how many were left out, and you can fill in the missing details by tapping that debt under Liabilities.

Pick any account to see its balance projected forward 30 days, built only from what's actually known and scheduled: that account's active subscriptions/bills and active income sources. It deliberately does not blend in an average-spending estimate - a forecast built only from real scheduled items is honest about what it knows, rather than looking more predictive than this app can actually claim to be. A bill or paycheck due today isn't shown as a separate point (it's already reflected in the starting balance); one due tomorrow or later shows up as a change in the line on the day it's expected.

Cards appear in this order, top to bottom. The month selector (top right), and the two totals that follow it, apply to everything except the last three cards - see below for which.

Most of this page shows whichever month you pick at the top right. The bottom three cards are the exception, because they cover your whole history rather than one month.

The three totals, each labelled with what it is: what you spent in the month you picked, how much of that was subscriptions, and a third that changes depending on what you have recorded.

"Of that, subscriptions" counts only spending you have written down and filed under Subscriptions in that month. It is not the same as the total on the Log page's Bills tab, which is what those plans cost you per month whether or not a charge has been logged. If you have subscriptions set up but none of their charges recorded yet, this reads $0.00 and says so underneath, rather than looking like the two pages disagree.

Before you have added any income, that third one reads Spent in a typical month - roughly what a normal month costs you. Once the app knows about any money coming in, it switches to Left over in a typical month: what you were paid, minus what you spent. A plus means you ended the month ahead, a minus means you spent more than came in. The label always changes with it, so it is never unclear which one you are looking at.

It waits for income before showing the left-over figure on purpose. With nothing recorded on the income side, that sum would read as a loss every month and tell you something untrue - so it shows the spending figure until it can answer the better question honestly.

That tile always describes now, not the month you picked, and it averages only over months that actually have something in them. If you have one month of history, it shows that month rather than quietly dividing by more months than you have data for. The small line underneath always says what it is based on, so a figure resting on very little is obvious rather than passing as settled.

It is worth knowing this can only count what you have actually written down. If you log some purchases and not others, it will read low - the fix is logging more, or importing a file from your bank.

Type a question in plain English (e.g. "How much did I spend on food last month?") and get an answer generated from your own real data - optional, and only works if this household has a Gemma server configured; otherwise it tells you plainly that it isn't set up rather than guessing. It can also draw on your Profile's financial-context fields (housing, employment, goals) if you've filled those in, for a more useful answer to something like "should I pay down debt or save right now?" - it never gives investment advice, only reasons about the numbers you've actually entered.

Questions can also reach further back than the usual recent-months data: a background search looks through your older transactions for whatever specifically relates to your question (e.g. "what did I spend on my trip in 2019") and adds just those to what the AI sees, on top of - not instead of - the regular recent-months data. A line under the answer says how many older transactions were found when this happens. Like the rest of this feature, it's optional and silently does nothing extra if it isn't set up yet.

A written summary generated on a schedule by a separate household server, not something you trigger from here - shows "No monthly reports yet" until that optional service has actually run at least once. The app is fully usable without it.

Every plain expense in the selected month (not account activity like balance corrections - this list is about spending specifically). Tap one to edit or recategorize it, same as Recent History on the Log page. Both ask you to confirm first: either one puts a readable copy of that month's spending somewhere outside the app, which matters on a shared or borrowed device.
